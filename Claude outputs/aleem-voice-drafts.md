## Lesson 1.1 — *It's a guessing machine*
**The moment:** AI handed you confident slop, your eye caught it.

> **A quick word from me.** My first product, Baytul Asmaa, started as slop. I typed my idea into Stitch and it handed me back something that *worked* — a clean card, a name, a meaning. I almost kept it. Then I looked again: it was treating a sacred name like a row in a spreadsheet. The machine was sure. It was wrong. My eye is what caught it — and yours will too, by the end of this.

*Hinge: "I almost kept it." That admission is what makes them trust you.*

## Lesson 1.2 — *Architect, not typist*
**The moment:** your architect origin — why "I can't code" doesn't stop anyone.

> I've been designing for over a decade, and I'll be honest with you — I don't sit and write the code myself. I decide how a thing should look, feel, and work, and I direct the build. That's the architect. When AI arrived, nothing changed for me except my draughtsman got faster. If you can picture what should exist, you're already doing the hard part. The typing was never the job.

*Hinge: "my draughtsman got faster." It reframes the fear in one image.*

---

## Lesson 1.3 — *Context is the machine's working memory*
**The moment:** how you actually feed your design system in as context.

> Here's a habit that changed everything for me. Before I ask the tool for anything, I hand it my design system first — my colours, my fonts, my rules — written down where it can see them. I don't *hope* it matches my style. I give it my style, up front, every time. Once you do this, the tool stops guessing like a stranger and starts building like it works for you.

*Hinge: "I don't hope it matches my style. I give it my style." The whole lesson in two sentences.*

---

## Lesson 1.4 — *Slow the start: brief before build*  ⭐ most personal
**The moment:** the honest confession — your impatience.

> Let me tell on myself. My biggest weakness as a builder is that I'm impatient — I get an idea and I want to see it on screen *right now*. So I rush the start. I skip the brief and jump straight to building. And every single time, it costs me. I've learned the hard way that five slow minutes at the front save an hour of undoing at the back. If you're impatient like me — this is the lesson I most need you to hear.

*Hinge: "Let me tell on myself." A trainer admitting his own trap is the most disarming line in the whole track.*

## Lesson 1.5 — *Taste is the last mile* (five slots)

### A · The opener
**The moment:** you knew a working thing still wasn't right.

> The first version of Baytul Asmaa worked perfectly. And I couldn't stand it. Everything functioned — and none of it felt like anything. That feeling, the one that won't let you ship something that merely works, is the most valuable thing I own. Let me show you where it comes from.

*Hinge: "worked perfectly. And I couldn't stand it."*

### B · The worked example (the taste loop on your own product)

> So here's what I actually did. I wrote one sentence down: *this should feel like an old manuscript, not an app.* Then I stopped asking for "nicer" and started directing — an editorial serif for the name, real Arabic calligraphy, parchment, thin gold lines. Stitch, then Figma, then back to Stitch. Pass after pass — rejecting what was average, keeping what was mine — until the screen finally felt the way that sentence said.

*Hinge: "I stopped asking for 'nicer' and started directing." That's the move you're teaching.*

### C · The reveal (your half-pixel eye)

> People ask how I know when something's off. Honestly? I can feel a half-pixel gap before I can measure it — spacing that's two pixels wrong will bother me from across the room. That's not a gift I was born with. It's years of looking, on purpose. You're building the same eye right now, whether you feel it yet or not.

*Hinge: "It's years of looking, on purpose." It promises the eye is learnable — which is the sale.*

### D · The build demo (narrate one real loop, out loud)

> Watch me do one loop out loud. Here's the pass the machine gave me. Here's my sentence. Now look — this feels crowded, the name isn't the loudest thing, the calligraphy's fighting the serif. So I tell it exactly that, in plain words, and push again. See how *naming* the problem precisely is what fixes it? That's the whole skill.

*Hinge: "naming the problem precisely is what fixes it." Ties straight back to the design words.*

### E · The send-off

> Remember this when you leave: the machine will always get you to "it works." Whether it ever becomes "right" was always going to be up to you. That's not the boring part of building — that's the part with your name on it. Go make something only you would have made.

*Hinge: "the part with your name on it."*
